The Patchwork: How U.S. Privacy Law Actually Works

The United States has no comprehensive federal privacy statute, leaving a set of sectoral laws, the FTC's general authority over unfair or deceptive practices, and roughly twenty state regimes to cover the field. This brief maps that landscape and identifies where the practical risk sits, as distinct from the statutory one.

No single statute, by design

Most jurisdictions that take privacy seriously begin with one comprehensive law. The United States never did. Federal privacy law grew sector by sector instead, each statute written in response to a specific concern at a specific moment. The result is less a system than a map with overlapping borders, and a company's obligations depend on what data it holds, whose data it is, and where those people live.

The federal layer

At the federal level, obligations attach to particular kinds of information or particular kinds of business. The statutes most growing companies encounter are these:

  • HIPAA, for health information held by providers, health plans and the vendors that serve them.
  • The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, for consumer financial information held by financial institutions.
  • COPPA, for personal information collected online from children under thirteen.
  • The Fair Credit Reporting Act, for consumer reports and the decisions made with them.

Around these statutes sits the Federal Trade Commission, which uses its authority over unfair or deceptive practices to police privacy promises. In practice, a company's own privacy policy becomes a standard it can be held to.

The state layer

Since California enacted the CCPA in 2018, roughly twenty states have passed comprehensive consumer privacy laws of their own. They share a vocabulary of controllers, processors, sensitive data and consumer rights to access, correct and delete, but they differ on thresholds, exemptions, consent requirements and enforcement. Alongside them sit narrower state statutes on biometrics, health data and breach notification, some of which let individuals sue directly.

The statutory question is which laws apply. The practical question is which of them a counterparty will ask about first.

Where the practical risk sits

For a growing company, exposure rarely comes from an obscure provision. It tends to come from three places:

  1. Promises that no longer match practice, such as a privacy policy written for an earlier version of the product.
  2. Data collected without a clear purpose, kept without a retention schedule, or shared without a contract that says what the recipient may do with it.
  3. Sensitive categories, including health, biometric, precise location and children's data, handled under assumptions that hold in some states and not others.

These are the issues a buyer's or investor's diligence team is trained to find, and they are far easier to resolve before a transaction than during one.

Reading the map

The patchwork is not going away soon. Federal proposals have come and gone, and each new state law adds a variation rather than a replacement. The useful response is not to track every statute in the abstract, but to know what data the company holds, why it holds it, and which rules follow from that. Part Two of this series turns to exactly that question: what companies actually do with data.
